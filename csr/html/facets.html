3. Facets of Social Responsibility

Core Approaches, Carroll's Pyramid, Sensemaking, and Responsibility Spheres

3.1 Fundamental Approaches to CSR

Normative Approach

As members of society, companies are obligated to assume social responsibility[cite: 1]. In exchange for societal infrastructure, education, and security, social responsibility must be integrated into strategic management decisions[cite: 1].

Economic Approach

Focuses on demonstrating the business value generated by CSR activities[cite: 1]. CSR processes are aligned with corporate strategy to create value and competitive advantages[cite: 1].

Carroll's Four-Part Pyramid

Archie B. Carroll structured corporate responsibility into four distinct levels, ranging from basic economic survival to voluntary philanthropic engagement[cite: 1].

Figure 2: Carroll's Four-Part Pyramid (Carroll, 1991)[cite: 1]
1. Philanthropic Responsibilities

Be a good corporate citizen. Voluntary resources contributed to improve quality of life beyond societal expectations[cite: 1].

2. Ethical Responsibilities

Be ethical. Obligation to do what is right, fair, and avoid harm, going beyond legal demands[cite: 1].

3. Legal Responsibilities

Obey the law. Play by the rules of the game; legal compliance is required for survival[cite: 1].

4. Economic Responsibilities

Be profitable. The foundation upon which all other levels rest[cite: 1].

3.2 Basu & Palazzo CSR Sensemaking Model

Kunal Basu and Guido Palazzo introduced a process-oriented model analyzing how organizations interpret and structure their CSR commitments through cognitive, linguistic, and conative processes[cite: 1].

Cognitive Dimension
What firms think

Defines corporate identity orientation: Individualistic (maximization of self-interest), Relational (partnering with stakeholders), or Collectivist (viewing firm as part of community)[cite: 1].

Linguistic Dimension
What firms say

How CSR is justified (scientific, economic, ethical) and communicated transparently (balanced disclosure vs. one-sided positive publicity)[cite: 1].

Conative Dimension
How firms behave

Reflects corporate posture towards external feedback: Defensive (dismissing critique) vs. Proactive / Open engagement[cite: 1].

3.3 Spheres of Responsibility (Stefanie Hiss)

Stefanie Hiss classifies CSR into three practical spheres based on proximity to core business operations[cite: 1].

Figure 3: Categorization by Spheres of Responsibility[cite: 1]
  • Inner Sphere (Innerer Bereich): Direct core operational responsibilities (law compliance, profitability, fair working conditions)[cite: 1].
  • Middle Sphere (Mittlerer Bereich): Supply chain standards, ecological footprint, and stakeholder management along the value chain[cite: 1].
  • Outer Sphere (Äußerer Bereich): Philanthropic activities, corporate citizenship, donations, and community sponsorship[cite: 1].
